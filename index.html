    How to make an AI film that doesn't look like AI

    A short study guide based on four public Higgsfield AI YouTube videos, where a team made an 80-minute AI feature film in 14 days. You get their full workflow, step by step, with the mistakes they made and how they fixed them.

    ~800generations by one person in one day
    8shots from those 800 made the film
    72tries for one 10-second shot
    ~$400Kgeneration cost for the whole feature

    The one big idea

    AI filmmaking is not "type a prompt, get a film". It is a loop you repeat hundreds of times. The first try almost never works. Every failed try tells you what to fix in the next prompt.

    Describe to Claude→Get a detailed prompt→Generate a small batch→Look at what's wrong→Tell Claude the fix↺

    Two rules decide most of your success:

    • The images matter more than the prompt. A video copies the light, texture and mistakes of its reference images. Fix images first, because images are cheap and video is expensive.
    • Batch, then judge. If all takes fail the same way, the prompt is wrong. If the motion is right and only details break, keep batching the same prompt.

    The tools and what each one does

    You need two places: Claude writes everything, Higgsfield generates everything. A video editor puts it together.

    ToolUse it for
    Claude + skillsTurning the script into a shot list and writing every image and video prompt. Skills are reusable instruction files you upload once (shot list, acting, fights, lenses).
    Higgsfield Cinema StudioYour project home: folders per scene, all image and video models in one place.
    Collab projects & CanvasSharing with a team. Everyone sees the asset, its prompt and the tool used. Canvas is one board holding all final assets.
    Elements (@tags)Saving a character, outfit or place under a name like @bear, so it is attached every time you type the tag.
    Soul CinemaCheap, creative image model. Good for exploring looks and locations in big batches.
    Nano Banana ProImages from long, detailed prompts. Very consistent, but can look "plastic" without lighting words.
    GPT Image 2 / 2.5Editing an existing image and image-to-image work.
    Seedream 5.0 ProRealistic surfaces: skin pores, hair, fur, fabric. Also recolouring an image without changing the framing.
    AI CastFinding a face by type: age, era, archetype, budget.
    Seedance 2.0 / 2.5The video model. Also Draft mode (480p), Extend, Object Swap and new angles from a video.
    Seed Audio Voice ChangeSwapping a character's voice for a preset one. Cheap, so try several.
    Photoshop + any editorCollaging props, cutting the film, adding your own music and sound.
    Lesson 01

    Set up the project

    WhyA film has hundreds of assets. Without order you lose them and grab the wrong one.

    1. In Cinema Studio, create a project for the film.
    2. Make one folder per scene, with subfolders: characters, locations, props (outfits go in props).
    3. Make a Canvas and put only final, approved assets on it.
    4. Working with others? Share the project as a Collab and set permissions (viewer, collaborator, approval required).

    From the video: one teammate generated a whole scene with an old draft of a character's skin that was still on the canvas. The scene had to be redone. Keep the canvas clean.

    Lesson 02

    Write a style prefix

    WhyOne text block on top of every prompt keeps the whole film one look, even with 15 people generating.

    What to put in it

    1. Look: colour palette, framing, film texture.
    2. Light: "practical light only": sun, windows, lamps that are in the frame. Nothing else.
    3. Audio: "environmental sound effects only, no music, no subtitles".
    4. Frame rate: 24 fps.

    Do

    • Say where light comes from
    • Ban music in the generation, add your own later
    • Use a 60/30/10 colour split: 60% main colour, 30% second, 10% accent

    Don't

    • Let hidden light sources in: faces lit from nowhere look pasted in
    • Trust the model's music: Seedance gives one audio track, you cannot separate it later
    Lesson 03

    Turn the script into a shot list

    WhyA shot list cuts the story into small pieces (about 15 seconds each) that a video model can handle, and keeps every prompt in one place for later fixes.

    1. In Claude: Customize → Skills → upload the shot list skill.
    2. Start a chat, call the skill, upload the script, say which scenes you want, paste the style prefix.
    3. Phase 1: Claude splits scenes into shots with prompts.
    4. Phase 2: Claude lists the assets you need: characters, locations, props.
    5. Phase 3: after you make the assets, upload them and Claude maps them onto the prompts.
    6. Ask for the list as an HTML page with two columns: description on the left, prompt on the right.

    Do

    • Put "failure modes" (what must not happen) inside each prompt
    • Ask Claude to update only the one prompt you changed. They report it saves ~80% of tokens
    • Edit with "for prompt 21A: …"

    Don't

    • Re-render the whole list for one fix
    • Keep adding fixes to one prompt forever (see Lesson 07)
    Lesson 04

    Build characters

    WhyThe character must look the same in every shot. A sheet tells the model exactly who to draw.

    1. Describe the character to Claude in plain words. It writes the detailed prompt.
    2. Generate a character sheet: face on the left, full body on the right, plain grey background.
    3. Check up close. Skin patchy or plastic, hair "dead"? Run the sheet through Seedream with real reference photos for skin, hair and fabric. It's like texturing a 3D model.
    4. Make outfits as separate sheets, so clothes change without rebuilding the character.
    5. Save each as an Element with a tag (@bear).
    6. Make a video element: upload a short real voice recording ("This is how I look, and this is how I sound") plus a line on how the character moves.
    7. Long film? Keep one sheet per state: clean, injured, half-transformed. This is script supervising.

    From the video: the same line with an image element vs. a video element. The video element version sounds and moves like a real person, so fewer takes get thrown away.

    Do

    • Use AI Cast when you know the type but not the face
    • Cut the face from a wide shot of the sheet if the wide face looks off

    Don't

    • Animate a character that looks plastic: video makes it worse
    • Describe the face in the prompt when you attach a sheet. The model invents a new person
    Lesson 05

    Build locations

    WhyMost of the light and style in a video comes from the location image. A sloppy location gives a sloppy video.

    1. Describe the place to Claude: layout, windows, doors, what is where. Ask for a single view prompt.
    2. Generate in batches of 4 (21:9 for cinema).
    3. Don't read the long prompt. Look at the images and tell Claude what's wrong: "lost the hallway", "make the wall red".
    4. Repeat. The apartment in the video took 44 tries.
    5. When it's close, stop editing the same image again and again (it loses detail). Ask Claude for a fresh full prompt of that layout.
    6. Make a second view (for example, from the hallway looking in) as its own image.

    Prompt like a photographer

    Weak

    Small kitchen, table, chairs, window, plants, photorealistic, cinematic, ultra detailed.

    Strong

    High corner view looking down across the kitchen, 28mm lens, deep focus. Late evening, the only light is a warm lamp over the table and pale blue dusk through the window. Worn wooden table in the foreground, steam rising from a pot. Faint haze, soft film grain, deep shadows in the corners.

    Do

    • Shoot from 3/4 or a high corner ("CCTV")
    • Give every place an anchor (a tree, a bookshelf) to place people and camera
    • Add haze, grain and light words to fight the plastic look
    • Keep the frame simple. Clutter melts when it moves

    Don't

    • Shoot a location head-on. The model loses depth
    • Put front and back views in one image. The model mixes them up
    • Upload a city photo into the image model for a room. It may put the room outdoors

    Not sure what lens to name? Show Claude a still from a film you like and ask which camera and lens made it.

    Lesson 06

    Make props

    WhyProps carry story ("lore"): a photo wall says "these people were family" without words.

    1. Simple prop (a sticky note): a short prompt is enough.
    2. Prop with faces (a Polaroid): attach the character sheets and tag who is who.
    3. Many faces (a photo wall): generate each photo separately, then combine in Photoshop.
    4. Tricky shape (a monster claw): make a prop sheet showing it from outside and inside.

    From the video: one prompt for a wall of 12 Polaroids broke every face. Twelve separate images kept them right.

    Lesson 07

    Generate the video

    WhyThis is where the money goes. Smart testing saves most of it.

    1. Attach references in the order the prompt names them: image 1 = character, image 2 = location, and so on.
    2. Run Draft at 480p, batches of 4–8. You are only checking framing and who stands where.
    3. Skim each take. If the composition is wrong, skip it.
    4. All fail the same way? Fix the prompt in Claude. Motion right, details off? Batch more of the same prompt.
    5. Re-run only the winning takes at 1080p.

    Single shot or multi-shot?

    Use multi-shot (several cuts in one generation) for dialogue and action. It keeps light and positions consistent. Use single shot for everything else. If a single shot cuts by itself, tell Claude: "one continuous shot, don't split it into beats".

    Camera and space

    • Place the camera against the anchor: "camera right beside the fridge, fridge barely visible on the right edge of frame".
    • For hard layouts, ask Claude for a top-down map first, or mark start positions on an overhead frame and attach it as a "blocking reference".
    • Ask Claude to describe the room's layout in a "spatial layout block" inside the prompt.
    • One camera move per emotional shot. A push-in plus a rise plus a shake is too much.

    Do

    • When a prompt grows too long and contradicts itself, say "scrap it, start from scratch"
    • Screenshot the one good moment of a bad take and use it as the reference for the next
    • Use Extend to finish a shot that ends early
    • Generate new angles from a good take ("same moves, different camera")

    Don't

    • Keep stacking fixes on one prompt
    • Panic on a failed or blocked generation. Failed ones cost no credits; reword the prompt
    Lesson 08

    Direct the acting and dialogue

    WhyFlat, robot reactions are what make AI look like AI. Feeling and body detail fix it.

    Describe the feeling and the body, not just the action

    Weak

    Mara reads the letter and is sad.

    Strong

    Mara reads the letter from her brother and realises he is not coming home. She tries to stay calm in front of her son. Her eyes stop moving halfway down the page, her jaw tightens, she swallows hard, folds the letter too carefully and presses it flat with both hands. No tears yet.

    • Say what the character wants from the other person. The acting skill builds the performance around that ("he's trying to convince the interviewer, but doesn't believe it himself").
    • Small details sell emotion: "lower lip trembles", "walks so slowly it reads like being dragged by grief".
    • Dialogue: start with a wide establishing shot, give lines word for word, spell hard words by sound, leave room for pauses.
    • Fights: write them beat by beat (who, where, which move, how each one falls) and use the fight skill. Hide cuts in whip pans.
    • Voice: direct the performance first, then try preset voices in Voice Change and keep the one that fits.
    Lesson 09

    Edit as you go

    WhySeeing shots side by side with music tells you what is missing and which shot to fix next.

    1. Open a timeline with temp music before you generate.
    2. Drop each good take in right away and trim it.
    3. Cut on movement. Use an L-cut: the sound of one shot (a door closing) runs into the next.
    4. Missing a beat? Add a new shot to the list between two others ("a wide establishing shot between 21B and 21C").
    5. Review frame by frame. One bad frame can ruin a shot.
    6. Choppy motion (fps drop)? Step through, delete doubled frames. The audio breaks, so use it as B-roll.
    7. A failed take may still hold 1–2 good seconds. Cut and use them.
    8. Repeat the last line of one generation at the start of the next, so you can choose where to cut.

    Stuck on a shot? Move on and come back with fresh eyes. Unlike a real shoot, you can always regenerate.

    Lesson 10

    Work as a team

    WhyAfter many hours alone you stop seeing mistakes.

    • Work in pairs, at least two people per scene.
    • Pick a "slop director": one person who checks every shot before it goes in. "Slop" or "not slop".
    • Don't make one person the only director in a fast project. They won't have time to generate.
    • Share through Collab projects, not downloaded files, so the prompts travel with the images.
    • Keep every character state and location on one canvas everyone uses.

    Before you press Generate on a video

    • The style prefix is at the top of the prompt.
    • Reference images are clean: no plastic skin, no hidden light, no clutter.
    • References are attached in the order the prompt names them.
    • Camera position is tied to an anchor in the room.
    • Emotion and body detail are written, not just the action.
    • Dialogue is word for word; hard words spelled by sound.
    • "No music, no subtitles, 24 fps" is in the prompt.
    • Draft mode is on (480p) and the batch is small.

    Words to know

    BatchSeveral takes from one prompt, run at once.
    SlopA take that looks fake: morphing, plastic skin, wrong layout.
    Keyframe / referenceThe image the video starts from or copies.
    Style prefixShared text on top of every prompt for one look.
    ElementA saved asset you call with a tag like @bear.
    AnchorA clear object in a location used to place camera and people.
    Establishing shotA wide shot that shows where everyone is.
    L-cutSound from one shot carries over the next shot.
    B-rollExtra shots cut into the main scene, often silent.
    Script supervisingTracking each character's look, injuries and props across scenes.
